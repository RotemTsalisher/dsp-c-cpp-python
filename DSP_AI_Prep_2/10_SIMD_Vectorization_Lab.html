10 — SIMD & Vectorization Laboratory

From scalar loops to dependency-aware vector kernels

SIMD from first principles

SIMD means a vector instruction applies the same operation to multiple independent lanes. The interview question is rarely “define SIMD”; it is usually “why does this code vectorize, why doesn't it, and what happens to the memory system?”

Dependency chains

float s = 0;
for (int i=0;i<n;i++)
    s += a[i] * b[i];

The multiplication is independent per iteration, but the single accumulator creates a serial reduction dependency. Multiple accumulators can expose parallelism:

float s0=0,s1=0,s2=0,s3=0;
for(int i=0;i+3<n;i+=4){
    s0 += a[i]*b[i];
    s1 += a[i+1]*b[i+1];
    s2 += a[i+2]*b[i+2];
    s3 += a[i+3]*b[i+3];
}
float s=s0+s1+s2+s3;

However, changing floating-point addition order can change the numerical result.

Exercise 10.1

Why can four accumulators help even before SIMD is introduced?

They break one long dependency chain into several shorter chains. A processor can potentially overlap independent multiplies/adds and hide some instruction latency. The exact benefit depends on the architecture and compiler.

Exercise 10.2

Why does unrolling by 4 not guarantee a 4× speedup?

The loop may already be memory-bound, the compiler may already unroll/vectorize, instruction issue limits may dominate, cache behavior may not improve, and tail/control overhead may be small compared with the main work. Optimization must be measured.

Exercise 10.3

A vector width is 8 floats. n=100. How many full vector iterations and scalar tail elements exist?

100/8 = 12 full vectors, consuming 96 elements, leaving 4 tail elements.

Exercise 10.4

Give two strategies for handling the SIMD tail. Discuss one advantage and one risk of each.
  • Scalar cleanup: process the remainder with scalar code. Simple and safe; adds a small separate path.
  • Masked vector operation: load/store only valid lanes. Can avoid scalar code but requires ISA support and careful masking to prevent invalid memory accesses.

Exercise 10.5

Why can alignment matter? Why should you avoid claiming “unaligned is always slow”?

Some architectures/instructions have alignment requirements or perform differently for aligned/unaligned accesses. Modern CPUs may handle many unaligned accesses efficiently when they remain within cache lines, while crossings or particular instructions can be expensive. Therefore the correct statement is architecture-dependent: know the target and measure.

Exercise 10.6

Design a vectorizable FIR loop. What prevents naïve vectorization in some FIR implementations?

A straightforward FIR inner loop has independent products for each tap and can often vectorize across taps, but issues arise from boundary handling, coefficient/input layout, feedback dependencies in IIR filters, alignment, aliasing, and the desired output layout. Choosing whether to vectorize across taps, samples, or channels depends on the workload and data organization.

Exercise 10.7

You vectorize a floating-point reduction and see 2× speedup but 1e-5 maximum absolute error. What questions do you ask before accepting it?
  1. What is the required numerical tolerance?
  2. Is the error absolute, relative, or signal-to-noise based?
  3. Does the error accumulate across blocks?
  4. Are there pathological inputs?
  5. Did compiler flags change IEEE behavior?
  6. Does the optimized result remain stable in the full pipeline?
